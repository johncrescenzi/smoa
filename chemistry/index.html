Objective 1

What is stoichiometry?

Stoichiometry (stoy-kee-OM-uh-tree) is the part of chemistry that studies the amounts of reactants and products in a chemical reaction.

A recipe

A recipe tells you how much of each ingredient you need. If you double the ingredients, you double the food.

A balanced equation

A balanced equation is a chemistry recipe. The coefficients tell you how many particles (or moles) react and form.

Why it matters

Factories, medicine and cooking all need to know: how much do I need? and how much will I make?

Objective 1

Reading a balanced equation

Read it as…2H₂+ O₂→ 2H₂O
🔵 particles (molecules)2 molecules1 molecule2 molecules
📦 moles2 mol1 mol2 mol
⚖️ mass (mol × molar mass)2 × 2.02 = 4.04 g1 × 32.00 = 32.00 g2 × 18.02 = 36.04 g
Look: 4.04 g + 32.00 g = 36.04 g. Mass is conserved. But the coefficients are moles, not grams. Never use coefficients with grams.
✎ Notebook page 1 · What is stoichiometry?
  • Stoichiometry = the study of the amounts of reactants and products in a reaction.
  • A balanced equation is like a recipe.
  • Coefficients = number of particles or number of moles. They are NOT grams.
  • 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O means 2 mol H₂ + 1 mol O₂ → 2 mol H₂O.
  • Mass is conserved: 4.04 g + 32.00 g = 36.04 g.
Objectives 2 and 3

The mole ratio

A mole ratio is a conversion factor that relates the amounts in moles of any two substances in a chemical reaction. You get it from the coefficients of the balanced equation.

Why it is important: the mole ratio is the bridge between two different substances. Every stoichiometry problem uses it. You cannot go from substance A to substance B without it.
Objective 3

Writing and using a mole ratio

Put the substance you want on top. Put the substance you have on the bottom, so its unit cancels.

Example: 2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂

Have mol Al₂O₃, want mol Al:

4 mol Al2 mol Al₂O₃

Have mol Al₂O₃, want mol O₂:

3 mol O₂2 mol Al₂O₃

Units cancel

5.0 mol Al₂O₃ × 4 mol Al2 mol Al₂O₃ = 10 mol Al

The "mol Al₂O₃" on top and bottom cancel. Only "mol Al" is left.

Remember: wanted on top, given on the bottom. Use the equation's coefficients only.
✎ Notebook page 2 · Mole ratios
  • Mole ratio = a conversion factor between the moles of any two substances in a reaction.
  • It comes from the coefficients of the balanced equation.
  • N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ gives: 1 mol N₂ / 3 mol H₂ · 2 mol NH₃ / 1 mol N₂ · 2 mol NH₃ / 3 mol H₂ (and each flipped).
  • Wanted on top, given on the bottom, so units cancel.
  • It is the bridge from substance A to substance B.

Objective 5

Mole → mole calculations

Example

4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃. How many moles of Al₂O₃ form from 6.0 mol Al?

1
Given: 6.0 mol Al. Wanted: mol Al₂O₃.
2
Mole ratio (wanted / given): 2 mol Al₂O₃4 mol Al
3
6.0 mol Al × 2 mol Al₂O₃4 mol Al = 3.0 mol Al₂O₃

Try it together

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. How many moles of O₂ react with 8.0 mol H₂?

Show answer
8.0 mol H₂ × 1 mol O₂2 mol H₂ = 4.0 mol O₂
✎ Notebook page 3 · Mole → mole
  • Map: mol A → × mole ratio → mol B
  • Steps: (1) write given and wanted, (2) write the mole ratio (wanted on top), (3) multiply.
  • Example: 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃
    6.0 mol Al × (2 mol Al₂O₃ / 4 mol Al) = 3.0 mol Al₂O₃
Objective 6

Mole → mass calculations

Example

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. What mass of MgO forms from 2.0 mol Mg?

1
Molar mass of MgO = 24.31 + 16.00 = 40.31 g/mol
2
2.0 mol Mg × 2 mol MgO2 mol Mg × 40.31 g MgO1 mol MgO = 80.62 g MgO

Try it together

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. What mass of NH₃ forms from 1.0 mol N₂? (NH₃ = 17.04 g/mol)

Show answer
1.0 mol N₂ × 2 mol NH₃1 mol N₂ × 17.04 g1 mol NH₃ = 34.08 g NH₃
✎ Notebook page 4 · Mole → mass
  • Map: mol A → × mole ratio → mol B → × molar mass B → g B
  • Molar mass = add the atomic masses (g/mol). MgO = 24.31 + 16.00 = 40.31 g/mol.
  • Example: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
    2.0 mol Mg × (2 mol MgO / 2 mol Mg) × (40.31 g / 1 mol MgO) = 80.62 g MgO

Objective 7

Mass → mole calculations

Example

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. How many moles of H₂O form from 64.00 g O₂?

1
Grams → moles: divide by molar mass (O₂ = 32.00 g/mol)
2
64.00 g O₂ × 1 mol O₂32.00 g O₂ × 2 mol H₂O1 mol O₂ = 4.00 mol H₂O
Tip: "× 1 mol / molar mass" is the same as "÷ molar mass".
Objective 8

Mass → mass calculations

Example

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. What mass of MgO forms from 48.62 g Mg?

①
48.62 g Mg ÷ 24.31 g/mol = 2.000 mol Mg
②
2.000 mol Mg × (2 mol MgO / 2 mol Mg) = 2.000 mol MgO
③
2.000 mol MgO × 40.31 g/mol = 80.62 g MgO

All in one line:

48.62 g Mg × 1 mol Mg24.31 g Mg × 2 mol MgO2 mol Mg × 40.31 g MgO1 mol MgO
You can never go grams → grams directly. Always go through moles: g → mol → mol → g.

✎ Notebook page 5 · Mass → mole, mass → mass

  • Mass → mass: g A → mol A → mol B → g B (3 steps ① ② ③)

  • Example: 48.62 g Mg → 2.000 mol Mg → 2.000 mol MgO → 80.62 g MgO
Objective 9

Limiting reactants: the sandwich shop

Recipe: 2 bread + 1 cheese → 1 sandwich. Move the sliders.

Limiting reactant

The reactant that is used up first. It limits (stops) the amount of product.

Excess reactant

The reactant that is left over when the reaction stops.

The limiting reactant is not always the one with less! You must compare using the recipe (the mole ratio).
Objective 9

How to find the limiting reactant

Method: "Which makes less product?"

1
Change both reactants to moles (if given in grams).
2
For each reactant, calculate the moles of product it could make.
3
The reactant that makes the smaller amount is the limiting reactant. That smaller amount is the answer.

Molecule view: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Example

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O with 4.0 mol H₂ and 3.0 mol O₂.

4.0 mol H₂ × 2 mol H₂O2 mol H₂ = 4.0 mol H₂O
3.0 mol O₂ × 2 mol H₂O1 mol O₂ = 6.0 mol H₂O

H₂ makes less → H₂ is limiting. O₂ is in excess. Product = 4.0 mol H₂O

Excess left: H₂ uses 4.0 × (1/2) = 2.0 mol O₂. 3.0 − 2.0 = 1.0 mol O₂ left over.

Objective 10

Limiting reactant with masses

2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl. You have 46.0 g Na and 50.0 g Cl₂. What mass of NaCl forms?

1
Moles: 46.0 g ÷ 22.99 = 2.00 mol Na · 50.0 g ÷ 70.90 = 0.705 mol Cl₂
2
Na: 2.00 mol × (2 mol NaCl / 2 mol Na) = 2.00 mol NaCl
Cl₂: 0.705 mol × (2 mol NaCl / 1 mol Cl₂) = 1.41 mol NaCl
3
Cl₂ makes less → Cl₂ is limiting, Na is in excess.
4
Mass: 1.41 mol × 58.44 g/mol = 82.4 g NaCl
Notice: there were more grams of Cl₂ (50.0 g) than Na (46.0 g), but Cl₂ is still limiting. Always compare in moles!
✎ Notebook page 6 · Limiting reactants
  • Limiting reactant: used up first; decides how much product forms.
  • Excess reactant: some is left over.
  • Method: (1) change to moles, (2) calculate product from each reactant, (3) the smaller product amount wins; that reactant is limiting.
  • Example: 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl, 46.0 g Na and 50.0 g Cl₂ → Cl₂ limiting → 82.4 g NaCl.
  • Compare in moles, never in grams.

Objective 11

Theoretical, actual and percentage yield

Theoretical yield

The maximum amount of product that can form, calculated from the limiting reactant (with stoichiometry).

Actual yield

The amount of product you really get in the lab (measured). Usually less than the theoretical yield.

Percentage yield

Compares actual to theoretical:
% yield = actual yieldtheoretical yield × 100

Why is actual yield smaller? Some product is lost (spilled, stuck to glass), reactions do not finish, impure reactants, side reactions make other products.

Objective 12

Calculating percentage yield

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. A student heats 100.09 g CaCO₃ and gets 50.0 g CaO. What is the percentage yield?

1
Theoretical yield (mass → mass):
100.09 g ÷ 100.09 g/mol = 1.000 mol CaCO₃ → 1.000 mol CaO → × 56.08 = 56.08 g CaO
2
Actual yield = 50.0 g (given)
3
% yield = 50.0 g56.08 g × 100 = 89.2%
✎ Notebook page 7 · Yield
  • Theoretical yield = maximum product, calculated from the limiting reactant.
  • Actual yield = product really measured in the lab.
  • % yield = (actual ÷ theoretical) × 100
  • Actual is usually less: product lost, reaction not complete, side reactions, impure reactants.
  • Example: theoretical 56.08 g CaO, actual 50.0 g → 89.2%
