Kinetic-molecular theory

Matter is made of moving particles

The kinetic-molecular theory (KMT) says: all matter is made of tiny particles that are always moving. "Kinetic" means motion. The theory explains the properties of solids, liquids and gases.

Kinetic-molecular theory

The 5 assumptions of the KMT for gases

Memory trick: Tiny · Elastic · Random motion · No attraction · Temperature = KE
Kinetic-molecular theory

Temperature and kinetic energy

KE = ½ m v²
  • m = mass of a particle · v = speed
  • Temperature measures the average KE of the particles.
  • Higher temperature → particles move faster.
  • At the SAME temperature, all gases have the same average KE.
So: at the same temperature, lighter particles move faster. H₂ (2 g/mol) is faster than O₂ (32 g/mol).
✎ Notebook page 1 · Kinetic-molecular theory
  • KMT: matter is made of particles that are always moving.
  • 5 assumptions for gases: 1) tiny and far apart 2) elastic collisions (no KE lost) 3) constant, rapid, random motion 4) no attractions 5) temperature = average KE.
  • KE = ½mv². Higher T → faster particles.
  • Same T → same KE → lighter particles move faster.

Six properties of gases

Every property comes from the KMT: particles are far apart (low density, compressible), move fast and randomly (expansion, diffusion), and do not attract (fluidity).

Diffusion and effusion

✎ Notebook page 2 · Properties of gases
  • Expansion: fills the whole container.
  • Fluidity: flows; particles slide past each other.
  • Low density: about 1/1000 of a liquid.
  • Compressibility: can be squeezed (lots of empty space).
  • Diffusion: spreads from high to low concentration.
  • Effusion: passes through a tiny hole.
  • Lighter gases diffuse and effuse faster.

Gases

Ideal gases and real gases

Ideal gas

An imaginary gas that fits ALL 5 KMT assumptions perfectly.

Real gas

A gas that does not behave completely like an ideal gas. All real gases act almost ideal at normal conditions.

Phases

Why are there 3 phases?

Two things fight: kinetic energy (moves particles apart) vs attractive forces (pull particles together).

Low energy: attractions win → solid. Medium: particles move but stay close → liquid. High energy: KE wins → gas.

Solids

Solids

Particles vibrate in fixed positions. Attractions are strong.

  • Definite shape and volume
  • Definite melting point (crystalline solids)
  • High density, almost incompressible
  • Very slow diffusion

Crystalline solid

Particles in a regular, repeating pattern. Salt, ice, diamond.

Amorphous solid

Particles in a random arrangement. No definite melting point. Glass, plastic.

✎ Notebook page 3 · Liquids and solids
  • Phases = kinetic energy vs attractive forces.
  • Liquid: close particles slide past each other; definite volume; high density; incompressible; slow diffusion; surface tension.
  • Solid: particles vibrate in fixed places; definite shape and volume; definite melting point; high density; incompressible; very slow diffusion.
  • Crystalline = regular pattern. Amorphous = random (glass).

Phase changes

Six phase changes

Phase changes

Vaporization: liquid → gas

Vaporization = a liquid (or solid) changes into a gas. Two kinds: evaporation (at the surface, any temperature) and boiling (bubbles inside, at the boiling point).

Phase changes

Boiling point and air pressure

🏔️ High mountain

Less air pressure → water boils below 100 °C. Food cooks slower.

🍲 Pressure cooker

Higher pressure inside → water boils above 100 °C (about 120 °C). Food cooks faster.

📏 Normal boiling point

The boiling point at 1 atm. Water: 100 °C.

Phase changes

Freezing, melting and sublimation

💧➡️🧊
Freezingliquid → solid. RELEASES energy. Particles slow down and lock into place. Water freezes at 0 °C.
🧊➡️💧
Meltingsolid → liquid. ABSORBS energy. Melting point = freezing point for a pure substance.
🧊➡️♨️
Sublimationsolid → gas directly. Dry ice (CO₂), snow disappearing on a cold sunny day.
♨️➡️❄️
Depositiongas → solid directly. Frost on a window.
Real life: farmers spray water on fruit trees on frosty nights. As the water freezes it RELEASES energy, which keeps the fruit from freezing.

✎ Notebook page 4 · Phase changes

  • Evaporation cools: fastest particles leave.

  • Freezing: liquid → solid (releases energy). Melting: solid → liquid (absorbs).

  • During a phase change, temperature stays the same.

Energy for phase changes

q = n × ΔH
  • q = energy (kJ) · n = moles
  • ΔHfus (molar enthalpy of fusion) = energy to MELT 1 mol = 6.009 kJ/mol
  • ΔHvap (molar enthalpy of vaporization) = energy to VAPORIZE 1 mol = 40.79 kJ/mol
  • Freezing and condensing RELEASE the same amounts.

Example 1: moles

How much energy is needed to melt 3.00 mol of ice?

1
Melting → use ΔHfus
2
3.00 mol × 6.009 kJ1 mol = 18.0 kJ

Pressure

What is pressure?

pressure = forcearea
  • Gas particles hit the walls of their container. Each hit pushes. All the pushes together = gas pressure.
  • The air above us pushes down: atmospheric pressure. Higher up (mountains) → less air above → lower pressure.
  • A barometer measures atmospheric pressure. At sea level, air pushes mercury up a tube about 760 mm.

Dalton's law

Dalton's law of partial pressures

In a mixture, each gas pushes on its own: its partial pressure. The total pressure is the sum.

PT = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + …

Example

He 0.40 atm, Ne 0.25 atm, Ar 0.35 atm. Total?

0.40 + 0.25 + 0.35 = 1.00 atm
Dalton's law

Collecting a gas over water

Example

O₂ collected over water at 25 °C. Total = 99.5 kPa.

PO₂ = 99.5 − 3.17 = 96.3 kPa

Gas laws

Boyle's law: pressure and volume

At constant temperature, volume ↓ → pressure ↑ (inversely proportional).

P₁V₁ = P₂V₂

Why? Same particles in less space → more hits on the walls. Examples: 💉 pushing a syringe; 🤿 air bubbles get BIGGER as they rise in water (less pressure on them).

Example

2.40 L of gas at 98.0 kPa is compressed to 1.20 L. New pressure?

P₂ = P₁V₁V₂ = 98.0 kPa × 2.40 L1.20 L = 196 kPa
Gas laws

Charles's law: volume and temperature

At constant pressure, temperature ↑ → volume ↑ (directly proportional). Temperature must be in kelvin! Examples: 🎈 a balloon shrinks in the freezer; 🔥 air in a hot-air balloon expands when heated.

V₁T₁ = V₂T₂

Example

2.00 L of gas at 27 °C is heated to 177 °C. New volume?

1
T₁ = 27 + 273 = 300 K · T₂ = 177 + 273 = 450 K
2
V₂ = V₁T₂T₁ = 2.00 L × 450 K300 K = 3.00 L

The line points to absolute zero (0 K = −273 °C), where gas volume would be zero.

Gas laws

Gay-Lussac's law: pressure and temperature

At constant volume, temperature ↑ → pressure ↑ (directly proportional). Examples: 🥫 a spray can in a fire; 🚗 tire pressure rises after a long drive.

P₁T₁ = P₂T₂

Example

A gas at 1.20 atm and 22 °C is heated to 85 °C. New pressure?

P₂ = 1.20 atm × 358 K295 K = 1.46 atm
✎ Notebook page 7 · Gas laws
  • Boyle (T constant): P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. V↓ P↑.
  • Charles (P constant): V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂. T↑ V↑.
  • Gay-Lussac (V constant): P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂. T↑ P↑.
  • ALWAYS use kelvin: K = °C + 273.
  • Absolute zero = 0 K = −273 °C.

Gas laws

The combined gas law

When P, V and T all change, use:

P₁V₁T₁ = P₂V₂T₂

If one quantity stays the same, cross it out: you get Boyle, Charles or Gay-Lussac.

Example

1.00 L of gas at 25 °C and 100. kPa. What is the volume at STP (0 °C, 101.3 kPa)?

1
T₁ = 298 K, T₂ = 273 K
2
V₂ = P₁V₁T₂T₁P₂ = 100. × 1.00 L × 273298 × 101.3 = 0.904 L
Tip: write a table: P₁ V₁ T₁ | P₂ V₂ T₂. Put a ? on the unknown.
Volumes and moles

The law of combining volumes

At the same T and P, the volumes of gases in a reaction are in small whole-number ratios: the same as the coefficients.

Example

CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g). How many L of O₂ react with 5.0 L of CH₄?

5.0 L CH₄ × 2 L O₂1 L CH₄ = 10. L O₂
Volumes and moles

Avogadro's law and molar volume

Avogadro's law: equal volumes of gases at the same T and P contain equal numbers of molecules. So V ∝ n: double the moles → double the volume.

Why it matters: it explains the law of combining volumes, and it lets us count molecules by measuring volume.

1 mol of ANY gas at STP = 22.4 L

Example

Volume of 3.50 mol of N₂ at STP?

3.50 mol × 22.4 L1 mol = 78.4 L
✎ Notebook page 8 · Volumes and moles
  • Combined gas law: P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂.
  • Law of combining volumes: gas volumes in reactions = small whole-number ratios (the coefficients).
  • Avogadro's law: equal volumes, same T and P → equal numbers of molecules. V ∝ n.
  • Molar volume at STP = 22.4 L/mol.

Ideal gas law

The ideal gas law

PV = nRT
  • P = pressure · V = volume (L) · n = moles · T = temperature (K)
  • R = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K) when P is in atm
  • R = 8.314 L·kPa/(mol·K) when P is in kPa
  • Use it when there is ONE set of conditions (no "1" and "2").
It combines all the laws: Boyle (P ∝ 1/V), Charles (V ∝ T), Gay-Lussac (P ∝ T) and Avogadro (V ∝ n).

Example 1: find P

2.00 mol of gas in 10.0 L at 300 K.

P = nRTV = 2.00 × 0.0821 × 30010.0 = 4.93 atm

Example 2: find n

3.00 L of gas at 1.20 atm and 27 °C.

n = PVRT = 1.20 × 3.000.0821 × 300 = 0.146 mol

Try it together

Volume of 0.500 mol of gas at 1.00 atm and 273 K?

Show answer
V = 0.500 × 0.0821 × 273 ÷ 1.00 = 11.2 L (half of 22.4 ✓)
✎ Notebook page 9 · Ideal gas law
  • PV = nRT
  • R = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K) or 8.314 L·kPa/(mol·K)
  • V in L, n in mol, T in K.
  • Solve: P = nRT/V · V = nRT/P · n = PV/RT · T = PV/nR
  • Grams? Change to moles first (÷ molar mass).
