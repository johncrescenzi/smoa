Topic 3.1

Generalist and specialist species

A population = all the members of ONE species living in one area. A species' niche = its role: what it eats, where it lives, how it survives.

Topic 3.1

Broad niche vs narrow niche

Generalists

Raccoons, rats, cockroaches. They do well in changing environments and are often invasive.

Specialists

Koalas (eucalyptus), giant pandas (bamboo), monarch caterpillars (only milkweed). They do well in stable environments but are at risk if their food or habitat disappears.

Topic 3.2

K-selected and r-selected species

Topic 3.2

r vs K: the details

✎ Notebook page 1 · 3.1 and 3.2
  • Generalist: broad niche; does well when the environment changes; often invasive.
  • Specialist: narrow niche; does well in a stable environment; higher extinction risk.
  • r-selected: many small offspring, little care, early maturity, short life; populations boom and crash; recover fast; often invasive.
  • K-selected: few large offspring, lots of care, late maturity, long life; population near K; recover slowly.
  • Biotic potential = max growth rate in ideal conditions.

Topic 3.3

Survivorship curves

A survivorship curve shows how many individuals of a group born at the same time are still alive at each age.

Topic 3.3

Three types of survivorship curves

✎ Notebook page 2 · 3.3 Survivorship curves
  • Survivorship curve = how many are still alive at each age.
  • Type I: most survive to old age (humans, elephants). K-selected.
  • Type II: equal chance of dying at every age (songbirds, squirrels, lizards).
  • Type III: most die young (fish, insects, sea turtles, plants). r-selected.
  • The y-axis is a log scale (1, 10, 100, 1000).

Topic 3.4

Carrying capacity

Carrying capacity (K) = the largest population an environment can support for a long time. It is set by limiting resources: food, water, space, shelter.

Topic 3.4

✎ Notebook page 3 · 3.4 Carrying capacity
  • Carrying capacity (K) = largest population an environment can support for a long time.
  • Set by limiting resources: food, water, space.
  • Overshoot: above K → resources depleted → dieback.
  • Overshoot damages the ecosystem and can lower K.
  • Example: St. Matthew Island reindeer: 29 (1944) → ~6,000 (1963) → 42 (1966).

Topic 3.5

Exponential growth

Topic 3.5

Resources limit growth

🍎💧🏠🦠 Density-dependent factors

Get STRONGER as the population gets more crowded: competition for food, water and space; disease; predators.

🌀🔥❄️ Density-independent factors

Affect the population no matter how crowded it is: hurricanes, fires, floods, very cold winters.

When resources are unlimited → exponential growth (J-curve). When resources are limited → logistic growth (S-curve): growth slows and levels off at K.

Topic 3.5

Calculating exponential growth

N = N₀ ert
  • N = population after time t
  • N₀ = starting population
  • r = growth rate (0.10 = 10% per year)
  • t = time · e ≈ 2.718 (calculator key eˣ)

Example

100 rabbits, r = 0.10 per year. How many after 10 years?

1
rt = 0.10 × 10 = 1.0
2
e1.0 = 2.718
3
N = 100 × 2.718 = 272 rabbits

Topic 3.6

Age structure diagrams

An age structure diagram shows how many people are in each age group: males on one side, females on the other. Each bar = one age group (youngest at the bottom).

Topic 3.6

How an age structure changes over time

Topic 3.6

Real examples

🔺 Rapid growth

Niger, Nigeria, Afghanistan: many children, wide base.

▮ Slow / stable

United States, France: similar numbers in each age group.

🔻 Declining

Japan, Italy, South Korea: few children, many older people.

Population momentum: even if each family has fewer children, a country with a wide base keeps growing for many years, because so many young people are entering their reproductive years.
✎ Notebook page 5 · 3.6 Age structure
  • Age groups: 0–14 pre-reproductive · 15–44 reproductive · 45+ post-reproductive.
  • Wide base (pyramid) → rapid growth.
  • Straight sides → slow or stable.
  • Narrow base, top-heavy → declining.
  • Top-heavy → fewer workers support more retired people.

Topic 3.7

Total fertility rate (TFR)

TFR = the average number of children a woman has in her lifetime.

Replacement-level fertility ≈ 2.1: 2 children replace the 2 parents, plus a little extra because some children die before they can have children. Where more children die, replacement level is higher.

Infant mortality rate = the number of babies who die before age 1, per 1,000 live births.

  • Infant mortality rate = infant deaths per 1,000 live births.

Topic 3.8

Human population growth

1 billion (1804) → 2 (1927) → 4 (1974) → 8 billion (2022)

Why so fast?

  • 💉 Medicine and vaccines
  • 🚰 Clean water and sanitation
  • 🌾 More food from modern farming (the Green Revolution)

→ the death rate fell while birth rates stayed high.

Malthus (1798)

Thomas Malthus said human populations grow faster than food supplies, so famine, disease and war would limit them. Technology has raised Earth's carrying capacity for humans so far, but people still debate how many humans Earth can support.

Topic 3.8

Growth rate and doubling time

A population grows when births + immigration > deaths + emigration, and declines when deaths + emigration > births + immigration.

Example 1: growth rate

CBR = 30, CDR = 10 (per 1,000), no migration.

30 − 1010 = 2.0%

Example 2: rule of 70

Growth rate = 2.0%. Doubling time?

702.0 = 35 years

✎ Notebook page 7 · 3.8 Human population dynamics
  • Human population: more than 8 billion (2022). Grew fast because the death rate fell (medicine, clean water, more food).
  • Malthus: population grows faster than food → famine, disease.
  • Growth rate (%) = [(CBR + immig.) − (CDR + emig.)] ÷ 10
  • Rule of 70: doubling time = 70 ÷ growth rate (%)

Topic 3.9

The demographic transition

The demographic transition = the change from high birth and death rates to low birth and death rates as a country develops economically.

✎ Notebook page 8 · 3.9 Demographic transition
  • Stage 1 pre-industrial: high BR, high DR → small, slow growth.
  • Stage 2 transitional: DR falls (medicine, food, water) → FAST growth.
  • Stage 3 industrial: BR falls (education, family planning, cities) → growth slows.
  • Stage 4 post-industrial: low BR, low DR → stable or declining.
