Topic 2.1

What is biodiversity?

Biodiversity = the variety of life in an area. We measure it at three levels:

Topic 2.1

Species diversity: richness and evenness

Species richness

The number of different species. Count the kinds, not the individuals.

Species evenness

How equal the numbers of each species are.

Answer: same richness (3 species). Meadow A is more even, so it has higher biodiversity.
Topic 2.1

Why does genetic diversity matter?

Every individual is a little different. If the environment changes (a new disease, heat, drought), some individuals may have genes that help them survive.

Rule: the more genetically diverse a population is, the better it can respond to environmental stressors (disease, climate change, pollution).
Topic 2.1

Population bottleneck

A population bottleneck = a population becomes very small for a time (because of a disaster, hunting or habitat loss). Many gene types are lost forever, even if the population grows back.

Topic 2.1

Biodiversity helps ecosystems recover

Ecosystems with more species are more likely to recover after a disruption (fire, storm, disease). If one species is lost, another can do its job.

When habitat is lost…

  1. Specialists are lost first (one food or habitat).
  2. Then generalists.
  3. Species that need a large territory also decline.
✎ Notebook page 1 · Topic 2.1 Biodiversity
  • Biodiversity = variety of life. 3 levels: genetic, species, habitat.
  • Richness = number of species. Evenness = how equal the numbers are.
  • High genetic diversity → better able to survive stressors (disease).
  • Bottleneck = population becomes very small → genetic diversity lost.
  • More species → ecosystem recovers better.
  • Habitat loss: specialists lost first, then generalists.

Topic 2.3

Island biogeography

Island biogeography = the study of how many species live on islands, and why.

🏝️ Real islands

Land surrounded by water: Hainan, Hawai‘i, the Galápagos.

🌳🏙️ Habitat islands

A habitat surrounded by a DIFFERENT habitat: a city park, a lake, a mountaintop, a forest surrounded by farms.

Topic 2.3

Two rules: size and distance

  • Size: bigger island → more habitats and food → bigger populations → fewer extinctions.
  • Distance: nearer island → easier for species to arrive → more immigration.
Topic 2.3

Simulator: immigration vs extinction

Immigration rate goes DOWN as the island fills up (fewer new species left to arrive). Extinction rate goes UP (more species compete). Where the lines cross = the equilibrium number of species.

Topic 2.3

Islands and evolution

Topic 2.3

Island species: special and in danger

🦤 Many endemic species

Island species are often found nowhere else. Many are specialists.

🐀🐍 Invasive species

Island species evolved without many predators. When humans bring rats, cats, snakes or goats, island species have no defenses. Example: brown tree snakes arrived on Guam and wiped out most of its native forest birds.

🦤 Extinction

The dodo (Mauritius) could not fly and was not afraid of people. It went extinct by about 1690.

Remember: islands → adaptive radiation (many new species) AND a high risk of extinction.
✎ Notebook page 3 · Topic 2.3 Island biogeography
  • Island = land in water OR a habitat surrounded by a different habitat.
  • Bigger island → more species (more habitats, fewer extinctions).
  • Nearer island → more species (more immigration).
  • Equilibrium: immigration line crosses extinction line.
  • Adaptive radiation: one species → many species, each fits a different niche (Galápagos finches).
  • Island species are at high risk from invasive species.

Topic 2.4

Ecological tolerance

Ecological tolerance = the range of conditions an organism can survive before it is injured or dies. Conditions include temperature, salinity (salt), water flow rate, sunlight and more.

Topic 2.4

Predict, then test it

Topic 2.4

Tolerance in real life

🪸 Coral bleaching

Reef-building corals live best at about 23–29 °C. When the ocean gets too warm, corals are stressed and push out their algae. They turn white (bleach). If it stays hot, they die.

🐟 Trout

Trout need cold water with lots of oxygen. Warm water stresses them, so they live in cool mountain rivers.

🧂 Salinity

Most freshwater fish cannot survive in salt water. Salinity is outside their range of tolerance.

Tolerance works for individuals AND species. One fish has a range of tolerance; a whole species has one too. Conditions outside the range limit where a species can live.
✎ Notebook page 4 · Topic 2.4 Ecological tolerance
  • Range of tolerance = conditions an organism can survive (temperature, salinity, flow, sunlight).
  • Optimal range: grows and reproduces best.
  • Zone of physiological stress: survives, but grows and reproduces less.
  • Zone of intolerance: dies.
  • Example: coral bleaching when water is too warm.

Topic 2.5

Natural disruptions

Ecosystems are disrupted by nature too, not only by humans. Natural disruptions can be as big as, or bigger than, human disruptions. They can change huge areas of habitat.

Topic 2.5

Long-term change: ice ages and sea level

Topic 2.5

How wildlife responds: migration

Animals respond to disruptions by moving. Migration = moving from one area to another, often following food, water or the seasons.

  • Short-term: 🦬 wildebeest in East Africa follow the rains to find fresh grass every year.
  • Short-term: 🐦 birds fly south for the winter.
  • Long-term: 🦣 during ice ages, animals moved across land bridges to new continents.
If they cannot move or adapt, populations decline or die.
✎ Notebook page 5 · Topic 2.5 Natural disruptions
  • Natural disruptions can be as big as human ones.
  • Periodic = regular (seasons, tides). Episodic = now and then (El Niño, drought). Random = no pattern (volcano, asteroid).
  • Short-term: hurricane, fire, flood, drought, volcano.
  • Long-term: climate change over geologic time, ice ages.
  • Ice age → water frozen on land → sea level falls.
  • Wildlife responds by migrating.

Topic 2.6

Adaptations

Adaptation

A trait (body part, behavior) that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its environment.

Natural selection

Individuals with helpful traits survive and reproduce more, so the trait becomes more common.

Fitness

How well an organism can survive and reproduce. (Not how strong it is!)

Topic 2.6

Natural selection, step by step

Topic 2.6

How fast can a species adapt?

  • Organisms adapt over time through small changes in their genes.
  • Species with short generation times (bacteria, insects) adapt faster.
  • Adaptation can be short-term (many generations of insects in a few years) or long-term (thousands of years).
If the environment changes too fast (sudden or gradual), individuals must change their behavior, move (migrate), or die. If a whole species cannot adapt or move, it can go extinct.
✎ Notebook page 6 · Topic 2.6 Adaptations
  • Adaptation = trait that helps survival and reproduction.
  • Natural selection: variation → environment changes → helpful traits survive → passed on → become common.
  • Fitness = ability to survive and reproduce.
  • Example: peppered moths: soot → dark moths common.
  • Short generation time → faster adaptation.
  • Change too fast → change behavior, migrate or die (extinction).

Topic 2.7

Ecological succession

Ecological succession = the change in the species of a community over time, after a disturbance.

🪨 Primary succession

Starts on bare rock with no soil (new lava, rock left by a glacier).

🔥 Secondary succession

Starts where a disturbance removed the plants but the soil is still there (fire, hurricane, flood, abandoned farm).

Topic 2.7

Pioneer species and the climax community

🌱 Pioneer species

The first species to live in a new or disturbed area. They grow fast, need few nutrients and spread easily. Primary: lichens and mosses. Secondary: grasses and weeds.

🌳 Climax community

The stable, mature final stage. It changes slowly until the next big disturbance.

Topic 2.7

How succession changes an ecosystem

Remember: as succession goes on, biomass ↑, species richness ↑ (then levels off), soil gets deeper. NPP is highest in the middle stages.
✎ Notebook page 7 · Topic 2.7 Succession
  • Succession = change in species over time after a disturbance.
  • Primary: bare rock, no soil → lichens & mosses → grasses → shrubs → pines → climax forest.
  • Secondary: soil remains (fire, farm) → grasses → shrubs → pines → climax. Faster.
  • Pioneer species = first to arrive. Climax community = stable final stage.
  • Over time: biomass ↑, richness ↑, soil deeper. NPP highest in the middle.
