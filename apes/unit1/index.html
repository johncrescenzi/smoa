Topic 1.1

What is an ecosystem?

Ecosystem

All the living things in an area and the nonliving things they interact with.

Biotic

The living parts: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria.

Abiotic

The nonliving parts: sunlight, water, temperature, soil, air, nutrients.

Population · Community

Population = one species in one area. Community = all the populations in one area.

Resources

Living things need resources: food, water, light, space, nutrients and shelter. When a resource is limited (there is not enough), organisms must compete for it.

Big idea (ERT-1.A): how much of a resource is available changes how species interact. Fewer resources → more competition.
Topic 1.1

Five species interactions

InteractionEffectWhat happensExample
Competition− / −Two organisms use the same limited resource. Both lose some.lions and hyenas hunting the same zebras
Predation+ / −One organism eats another. Herbivory (an animal eats a plant) is a type of predation.wolf eats deer; deer eats grass
Mutualism+ / +Both species benefit.bees and flowers; coral and algae
Commensalism+ / 0One benefits. The other is not helped or harmed.barnacles on a whale; a bird nest in a tree
Parasitism+ / −The parasite lives on or in a host and harms it (usually does not kill it quickly).tick on a dog; tapeworm in a gut

Symbiosis = a close, long-term relationship between two species. Mutualism, commensalism and parasitism are all types of symbiosis.

Topic 1.1

Resource partitioning

When species compete for the same resource, over time they can evolve to use it in different ways. This reduces competition, so the species can live in the same place.

Remember: competition is the reason for resource partitioning. Partitioning lets species share a limited resource.
✎ Notebook page 1 · Topic 1.1 Ecosystems
  • Ecosystem = biotic (living) + abiotic (nonliving) parts of an area.
  • Competition −/− · Predation +/− (includes herbivory) · Mutualism +/+ · Commensalism +/0 · Parasitism +/−
  • Symbiosis = close long-term relationship (mutualism, commensalism, parasitism).
  • Resource partitioning reduces competition: temporal (time), spatial (place), morphological (body).
  • Fewer resources → more competition.
Topic 1.2

Terrestrial biomes: what decides a biome?

A biome is a large region with a similar climate and similar plants and animals. Two factors decide a biome: average yearly temperature and average yearly precipitation (rain + snow). Climate decides which plants can grow, and plants decide which animals can live there.

Topic 1.2

Where are the biomes? Soil and change

Global pattern (latitude)

  • Equator (0°): tropical rainforest, savanna
  • About 30° N and S: deserts (dry, sinking air)
  • 30°–60°: temperate forests, grasslands, shrublands
  • 50°–70°: boreal forest
  • Near the poles: tundra

Climbing a mountain is like moving toward the poles: it gets colder.

Soil nutrients

  • Tropical rainforest: poor soil. Plants take nutrients up fast and heavy rain washes the rest away.
  • Boreal forest: poor, acidic soil. Cold makes decomposition very slow.
  • Temperate seasonal forest: rich soil from fallen leaves.
  • Temperate grassland: deep, very fertile soil (good farmland).

Biomes can move

When the climate changes, biomes shift toward the poles and up mountains. Species that cannot move or adapt may die out.

✎ Notebook page 2 · Topic 1.2 Terrestrial biomes
  • Biome = climate (temperature + precipitation) → plants → animals.
  • Tundra: cold, dry, permafrost · Boreal forest: conifers, slow decay · Temperate rainforest: wet, giant conifers · Temperate seasonal forest: trees drop leaves, rich soil · Shrubland: dry summer, fires · Temperate grassland: grasses, fertile soil · Tropical rainforest: hot, wet, most species, poor soil · Savanna: grass + few trees, dry season · Desert: under 25 cm rain.
  • Deserts at about 30° N/S. Mountains: higher = colder.
  • Climate change → biomes move toward the poles.

Topic 1.3

Aquatic biomes: what decides them?

Water biomes are not decided by rain. They are decided by:

Salinity

How much salt is in the water.

Depth

How far sunlight can reach.

Flow

Moving water (rivers) or still water (lakes).

Temperature · oxygen · nutrients · turbidity

Turbidity = how cloudy the water is. Cloudy water blocks light.

Topic 1.3

Freshwater biomes

  • Littoral zone: shallow water near the shore. Rooted plants grow.
  • Limnetic zone: open water where light reaches. Phytoplankton live here.
  • Profundal zone: deep water, too dark for photosynthesis.
  • Benthic zone: the bottom (mud, sediment). Decomposers live here.

Rivers and streams

Flowing water. Fast, cold water holds more oxygen. Rivers carry nutrient-rich sediment and drop it at the mouth (a delta).

Wetlands

Land covered with water for part of the year: swamps (trees), marshes (grasses), bogs (acidic).

Why wetlands matter: they store floodwater, filter pollutants, give habitat, and are very productive.

Topic 1.3

Marine biomes

  • Photic zone: top layer with light. Phytoplankton make food and much of Earth's oxygen.
  • Aphotic zone: no light, no photosynthesis.
  • Benthic zone: the ocean floor.
  • Open ocean: low productivity per m², but a huge area.

Estuaries

Where a river meets the ocean. Brackish water. Rivers bring nutrients and the water is shallow and sunny, so estuaries are very productive. Salt marshes (temperate) and mangrove swamps (tropical) protect coasts and are nurseries for young fish.

Intertidal zone

Between high tide and low tide. Organisms must survive waves and drying out.

Coral reefs

Warm, shallow, clear water. Coral and algae live in mutualism. Highest biodiversity in the ocean. Warming water causes coral bleaching.

✎ Notebook page 3 · Topic 1.3 Aquatic biomes
  • Decided by salinity, depth, flow, temperature (also oxygen, nutrients, turbidity).
  • Freshwater: rivers, lakes, wetlands · Brackish: estuaries, salt marshes, mangroves · Marine: ocean, reefs, intertidal.
  • Lake zones: littoral (shore, plants) · limnetic (open, light) · profundal (deep, dark) · benthic (bottom).
  • Ocean: photic (light) · aphotic (dark) · benthic (floor).
  • Very productive: estuaries, wetlands, coral reefs. Wetlands store floods and filter water.
Topic 1.4

The carbon cycle

Carbon is in every living thing (sugars, proteins, DNA). The carbon cycle is the movement of carbon between reservoirs.

Reservoir = a place where carbon is stored.

Topic 1.4

Humans and the carbon cycle

Largest reservoirs

Sedimentary rock (largest) → ocean → fossil fuels → soil → atmosphere → plants.

Fast vs slow

Photosynthesis and respiration move carbon in days or years. Making rock and fossil fuels takes millions of years.

The problem

Burning fossil fuels and cutting forests move carbon from slow reservoirs to the atmosphere faster than sinks can take it back. CO₂ rises → global warming.

Ocean acidification

The ocean absorbs extra CO₂ and becomes more acidic. This harms corals and animals with shells.

✎ Notebook page 4 · Topic 1.4 Carbon cycle
  • Reservoir = store · Sink takes in more than it releases · Source releases more.
  • Removes CO₂ from air: photosynthesis, ocean dissolving.
  • Adds CO₂ to air: respiration, decomposition, combustion, volcanoes.
  • Slow: sedimentation (limestone), burial → fossil fuels (millions of years).
  • Largest reservoir: sedimentary rock. Humans burn fossil fuels → more CO₂ → warming + ocean acidification.

Topic 1.5

The nitrogen cycle

Living things need nitrogen to make proteins and DNA.

The atmosphere is 78% N₂, the largest nitrogen reservoir. But plants and animals cannot use N₂ gas: its triple bond is very strong. N₂ must first be fixed into a form plants can use (NH₄⁺ or NO₃⁻).

Nitrogen is often a limiting nutrient: there is not enough usable nitrogen, so plants grow slowly.

Topic 1.5

Nitrogen cycle steps

#StepWhat changesWho / how
1Nitrogen fixationN₂ → NH₃ / NH₄⁺ (ammonia / ammonium)bacteria in root nodules of legumes (beans, peas, clover) and in soil; lightning; the Haber-Bosch process (fertilizer factories)
2NitrificationNH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ (nitrite) → NO₃⁻ (nitrate)nitrifying bacteria in soil
3Assimilationplants take up NO₃⁻ (and NH₄⁺) through rootsplants build proteins and DNA
4Consumptionplants → animalseating
5Death and wasteorganisms → dead matter, urine, feces
6Ammonificationdead matter and waste → NH₃ / NH₄⁺decomposers (bacteria, fungi)
7DenitrificationNO₃⁻ → N₂O → N₂ gas (back to the air)bacteria in soil with little oxygen (waterlogged)
Order to remember: Fix → Nitrify → Assimilate → (eat, die) → Ammonify → Denitrify.
Topic 1.5

Humans and the nitrogen cycle

Fertilizer runoff

The Haber-Bosch process makes huge amounts of synthetic fertilizer. Extra nitrate runs off fields into rivers and lakes → eutrophication (algal blooms).

Leaching

Nitrate dissolves easily and moves down into groundwater. Too much nitrate in drinking water is unsafe.

Air pollution

Burning fuels releases NOx → smog and acid rain. Fertilized soil releases N₂O, a greenhouse gas.

The nitrogen cycle is faster than the phosphorus cycle because nitrogen has a gas phase in the atmosphere.

✎ Notebook page 5 · Topic 1.5 Nitrogen cycle
  • Largest reservoir: atmosphere (78% N₂). Plants cannot use N₂ until it is fixed.
  • Fixation N₂ → NH₄⁺ (bacteria in legume roots, lightning, Haber-Bosch)
  • Nitrification NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ (bacteria)
  • Assimilation plants take up NO₃⁻ / NH₄⁺
  • Ammonification dead matter → NH₄⁺ (decomposers)
  • Denitrification NO₃⁻ → N₂ (bacteria, low oxygen)
  • Humans: fertilizer runoff → eutrophication; leaching; NOx, N₂O.
Topic 1.6

The phosphorus cycle

  • Phosphorus is needed for DNA, ATP (energy), cell membranes, bones and teeth.
  • No gas phase. Phosphorus does not go into the atmosphere.
  • Main reservoir: rocks and ocean sediments.
  • Released slowly by weathering of rock → the slowest cycle.
  • Phosphate (PO₄³⁻) is often the limiting nutrient in water.
  • Humans add phosphorus by mining it for fertilizer, and from detergents and sewage.
Topic 1.6

Eutrophication: too many nutrients

Example: the Gulf of Mexico dead zone, from fertilizer carried by the Mississippi River.

Key reason: P (and N) were the limiting nutrients. Adding them removes the limit, so algae explode.
✎ Notebook page 6 · Topic 1.6 Phosphorus cycle
  • Needed for DNA, ATP, membranes, bones.
  • No gas phase · reservoir: rocks and sediments · slowest cycle.
  • Steps: weathering → soil/water PO₄³⁻ → plants → animals → decomposers → runoff → sediments → geologic uplift → rock.
  • Often the limiting nutrient in water.
  • Eutrophication: extra N, P → algal bloom → algae die → decomposers use O₂ → hypoxia → dead zone.

Topic 1.7

The hydrologic (water) cycle

Reservoirs

What drives it?

Energy from the Sun drives evaporation and transpiration. Gravity pulls water down: precipitation, runoff, infiltration.

Topic 1.7

Water cycle processes

#ProcessWhat happens
1Evaporationliquid water → water vapor (from oceans, lakes, soil)
2Transpirationwater vapor leaves plant leaves
3Condensationwater vapor cools → liquid droplets (clouds)
4Precipitationrain, snow, hail fall to Earth
5Runoffwater flows over land into rivers and the ocean
6Infiltrationwater soaks down into soil → groundwater
7Groundwater flowgroundwater moves slowly to rivers and the ocean
8Root uptakeplants take water from the soil

Evapotranspiration = evaporation + transpiration. An aquifer is underground rock or sediment that holds groundwater.

Humans: roads and parking lots are impermeable → more runoff, less infiltration, more floods. Pumping aquifers too fast lowers groundwater. Cutting forests lowers transpiration and rain.
✎ Notebook page 7 · Topic 1.7 Water cycle
  • Largest reservoir: ocean (97%). Most fresh water: ice caps and glaciers.
  • Sun drives evaporation and transpiration (together: evapotranspiration).
  • Condensation → clouds · precipitation → rain/snow.
  • Runoff over land · infiltration into soil → groundwater / aquifers.
  • Paving → more runoff, less infiltration.
Topic 1.8

Primary productivity

Producers (autotrophs) capture solar energy by photosynthesis and store it as chemical energy in glucose. Only about 1% of sunlight that reaches a plant is captured. The rest is reflected, passes through, or becomes heat.

GPP · Gross primary productivity

The total energy producers capture by photosynthesis.

R · Respiration

Energy producers use for their own life (cellular respiration).

NPP · Net primary productivity

Energy left after respiration. It becomes plant growth (biomass) and food for consumers.

Units: kcal/m²/yr or g/m²/yr. Example: GPP = 20,000 kcal/m²/yr and R = 12,000 → NPP = 20,000 − 12,000 = 8,000 kcal/m²/yr.

Topic 1.8

Measuring productivity in water: light and dark bottles

Light bottle

Photosynthesis and respiration happen. Change in dissolved O₂ = NPP.

Dark bottle

Only respiration happens (no light). O₂ lost = R.

Then

GPP = NPP + R

BottleStart O₂ (mg/L)After 24 hResult
Light8.09.5NPP = 9.5 − 8.0 = 1.5
Dark8.06.5R = 8.0 − 6.5 = 1.5
GPP1.5 + 1.5 = 3.0 mg O₂/L/day
Topic 1.8

Which ecosystems are most productive?

Why?

High NPP needs lots of sunlight, water, warm temperatures and nutrients. Deserts lack water; tundra is cold; the open ocean lacks nutrients.

Light in water

The open ocean has low NPP per m² but a huge total, because it covers most of Earth.

✎ Notebook page 8 · Topic 1.8 Primary productivity
  • Producers capture solar energy by photosynthesis (only about 1% of sunlight).
  • GPP = total captured · R = used by plant · NPP = GPP − R (available to consumers).
  • Light bottle change = NPP · dark bottle loss = R · GPP = NPP + R.
  • Highest NPP: swamps/marshes, reefs, tropical rainforest, estuaries. Lowest: desert, tundra, open ocean (per m²).
  • Blue light reaches deepest in water; red is absorbed first.

Topic 1.9

Follow the energy

Topic 1.9

Energy flows, matter cycles

➡️ Energy flows one way

Sun → producers → consumers → lost as heat. Energy does not cycle.

♻️ Matter cycles

Carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and water are reused. Decomposers return nutrients to the soil.

📜 Laws of thermodynamics

1st: energy is not created or destroyed, only changed in form. 2nd: every energy change loses some energy as heat.

Topic 1.10

The 10% rule

Only about 10% of the energy at one level passes to the next. About 90% is lost:

Going up one level: × 0.1 · Going down one level: × 10

Topic 1.10

10% rule calculations

Example 1: going UP

Producers have 20,000 kcal. How much reaches the tertiary consumers?

1
Primary: 20,000 × 0.1 = 2,000
2
Secondary: 2,000 × 0.1 = 200
3
Tertiary: 200 × 0.1 = 20 kcal

Example 2: going DOWN

A hawk (tertiary) gets 30 kcal. How much did the producers have?

1
30 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 30,000 kcal
So: there are few top predators, food chains are short (4–5 levels), and eating lower on the food chain feeds more people.

  • Energy flows one way (ends as heat). Matter cycles.
  • 1st law: energy is not created or destroyed. 2nd law: each transfer loses energy as heat.
  • 10% rule: about 10% passes up each level; 90% is lost (heat, not eaten, feces).
  • Up one level ×0.1 · down one level ×10.

Topic 1.11

Food chains and food webs

  • A food chain shows one path of energy: grass → rabbit → fox.
  • A food web shows many connected food chains.
  • Arrows point from the food to the eater: the direction energy flows.
  • Omnivores can feed at more than one level. The sparrow is a primary consumer when it eats berries and a secondary consumer when it eats grasshoppers.
  • Decomposers are usually not drawn, but they feed on every level.
Topic 1.11

Change one species, change the web

✎ Notebook page 10 · Topic 1.11 Food chains and webs
  • Food chain = one path · food web = many linked chains.
  • Arrow = energy flow, from food → eater.
  • Omnivores can be at more than one trophic level (sparrow).
  • Removing a species changes the whole web (no snakes → more mice → less grass).
  • All energy in the web starts with producers (and the Sun).
